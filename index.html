The RBI counts what a hitter did and what the eight men in front of him did. So count the second part, then divide it out. Every plate appearance below is scored for how many runners were standing on base — and how many of them the hitter sent home.

“RBIs are merely a byproduct of opportunity created by other players. Some guys have way more opportunities with RISP, so it’s unfair to say one player is better than another based on RBI.” The complaint this page answers

How This Was Counted

The denominator is real, not modeled

For each of the 40 hitters the page reads every plate appearance of the 2026 season from the MLB Stats API play log, and records the exact base state and out count at the deciding pitch. A man on first is one opportunity; bases loaded is three.

Totals reconcile: the per-base-state plate appearances add up to the official Runners On split, and the play log count matches the official plate-appearance total for every player.

Why RBI minus HR

A solo home run is an RBI with nobody on base — it would give a hitter credit with no opportunity to divide by. Every home run contains exactly one self-driven run, so RBI − HR is precisely the number of other runners a hitter sent home. Sluggers lose a little here; that is the point.

Does the stat already exist?

Partly. Baseball-Reference publishes baserunner counts inside its situational splits, so the raw pieces are on the internet. Nobody puts them on a leaderboard, nobody says them on a broadcast, and the two-out version is not published anywhere that shows up in a search.

Caveat: this is a rate over roughly 300–430 opportunities. That is a real sample, but not a career.
